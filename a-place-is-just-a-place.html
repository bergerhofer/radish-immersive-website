A Place Is Just a Place Until People Give It Meaning

Radish Immersive · 24 September 2026

A stadium is concrete, steel, seats, lights and a field. A museum is walls, objects, labels and glass cases. A brewery is tanks, taps, tables and chairs.

Those things create a place. But people create what that place means.

Think about a stadium after everyone has gone home. It’s technically the same stadium. The scoreboard is still there. The seats haven’t moved. The field is the same size. But something important is missing: the thousands of individual experiences that transform that collection of concrete and steel into our stadium.

Someone remembers sitting in Section 129 with their dad. Someone remembers the goal that sent everyone around them into complete chaos. Someone else remembers bringing their daughter to her first game. The physical place gives those experiences somewhere to happen. Humans give them meaning.

Context changes what we experience

We don’t experience places objectively. We bring our history, relationships, knowledge, expectations and emotions with us.

Put two people in front of the same object in a museum. One sees an old baseball glove. The other sees a glove used by a player their grandfather told them stories about when they were eight years old. It’s the same object, but a completely different experience.

The difference is context.

When a place understands that context, it has an opportunity to create something much more meaningful than an attraction, exhibit, event or transaction. It can create a connection between what exists in that place and something the person already cares about.

This is why context is such an important design material. A place can give you facts. Context helps you understand why those facts matter to you.

Technology can help, but it can’t be the point

For years, organizations have added technology to physical experiences because the technology itself seemed impressive. Touchscreens, apps, AR, projection, AI and interactive displays all promised to make places more engaging.

Some of those experiences are fantastic. Others leave you wondering why someone didn’t just put up a sign.

The better question isn’t, “What technology could we put here?” It’s, “What would help this person understand, feel or do something they couldn’t before?”

Maybe AI lets a museum visitor ask the question they actually care about instead of reading the same label as everyone else. Maybe a stadium recognizes that this is your kid’s first game and helps make it special. Maybe an exhibit changes based on what you’ve already discovered. Maybe a brewery helps you understand the people, ingredients and decisions behind the beer in your hand.

In each case, the technology isn’t the experience. It’s helping connect a person to the experience by providing the right context at the right moment.

Context creates meaning

This is a progression we keep coming back to at Radish:

CONTEXT → MEANING → CHANGE

Context helps me understand why something matters. Meaning creates a connection between that thing and me. That connection influences what I do next.

Maybe I stay longer. Maybe I tell someone about it. Maybe I buy something, come back, bring a friend or become a fan. Maybe I remember the experience ten years later.

That last one might be the most interesting outcome of all, because the best experiences don’t end when someone walks out the door. They become part of the stories people tell.

Design for the story someone will tell later

There’s a useful question for anyone designing a place or experience:

What story do we hope someone tells about this tomorrow?

That’s different from asking what information we want them to learn, how many screens we want them to touch or whether they downloaded the app. It forces us to think about the human experience instead of the mechanics of delivering it.

A museum becomes less about displaying information and more about creating moments of discovery. A stadium becomes more than a venue and starts becoming a collection of rituals, relationships and memories. A brewery becomes more than somewhere people drink beer and starts becoming part of a community.

Technology has a role in all of those places, but the goal isn’t to make the place feel more technological. The goal is to help people form a stronger relationship with it.

People finish the experience

Designers can create the architecture, build the exhibit, develop the software and choreograph the event. But we don’t get to decide what something means to another person.

We can give people the ingredients: context, discovery, participation, surprise, other people and a reason to care. Then they bring something we can’t manufacture: their own memories, relationships, curiosity and history.

That’s when a place stops being simply a place. It becomes the stadium where you went with your dad, the museum where your kid became obsessed with space, the brewery where your friends meet every Friday, or the neighborhood restaurant where everyone knows your name.

And eventually, the physical details almost become secondary. What lasts is the meaning people attached to them.

So here’s the harder question:

Are you designing a place for people to visit, or are you giving them something worth making part of their story?

At Radish, we design tech-enabled human experiences around that question. If you have a place, event or exhibit that people encounter but don’t yet connect with, we’d love to help figure out what’s missing.
